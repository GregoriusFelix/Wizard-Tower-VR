# a. Conclusion

This assignment helped me understand how to turn a fantasy-game idea into a mobile VR experience using Unity and Google Cardboard. I developed a wizard-tower environment where players navigate by gaze, collect firewood, interact with potions and magical objects, and cast elemental spells at a golem. Working within the geometry and material restrictions encouraged me to create recognisable objects through shape, scale and colour. I learned that a convincing environment also depends on how objects respond to the player, including their animation, sound and interaction feedback.

One important problem appeared in the initial Android build. Although the phone displayed stereoscopic images, the view appeared stuck and the centre indicator was difficult to see. This prevented the player from looking around and selecting objects reliably. To address this, I separated the player rig from the tracked camera, added rotation-only tracking and adjusted the Cardboard startup handling. Scripted movement and turning now act on the rig, while the camera remains responsible for head tracking. I also enlarged the mobile indicator. These changes passed C# compilation checks, but the latest build still needs device testing to confirm the complete result. This taught me that a working Unity preview and a successful compilation do not establish that the VR experience works correctly on a phone.

Another challenge was making gaze interactions understandable. The original staff options did not clearly show their selectable areas, and the Water and Air labels appeared off-centre. The Leave Arena button also lost its visible background because it was positioned too low. I addressed these issues by adding framed backgrounds around the staff options, adjusting the labels' position and depth, and raising the arena controls above the floor. I added instruction boards explaining the axe-to-cauldron and torch-to-spell sequences. From these changes, I learned that players need to recognise both where to look and what will happen after they select an object.

The golem's destruction also revealed a problem with visual believability. Its scattered stones appeared to float because their final positions and rotations did not consistently match the ground. I changed the scatter animation to follow gravity-based trajectories and calculated resting positions using the floor height and each stone's dimensions. This approach aims to make the stones land convincingly without adding dynamic physics to every piece. I also refined feedback elsewhere by matching the door handle's three movements to the recorded knocks and making the golem's impact sound easier to hear. These adjustments showed me how mismatched movement or sound can weaken an otherwise functional interaction.

If I developed the prototype again, I would begin with a small Android test containing only head tracking, one teleport pad and one gaze interaction. I would test this on the target phone before expanding the environment, then repeat device checks after major changes. This would help identify platform problems earlier and leave more time to resolve them. I would also profile the golem encounter and spell effects on the phone, then reduce unnecessary lights, particle counts and repeated object creation where measurements show a performance problem.

I would improve usability by observing new players attempting the main objectives without verbal help. I would record missed selections, accidental activations and places where players become unsure, then use those observations to adjust dwell times, text size and instructions. An optional objective panel could show the next step without requiring players to return to a sign. Finally, I would give the elemental spells different effects on the encounter, rather than mainly different visual presentations, so that choosing a staff creates a more meaningful gameplay decision. These improvements would strengthen the prototype as a mobile VR adventure while keeping its scope manageable.
